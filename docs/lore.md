# TIME TO LIVE: the lore of the voyage

This is the canon for *TIME TO LIVE — a Faultline voyage*. It sits on top of FAULTLINE's canon
(`/home/clab/projects/clab/faultline/docs/lore.md`), which stays true in every line, and follows the contract's
**Direction v2** (cable tenders on the carriers, three mobility classes of hostiles). Where this document adds
something, it adds; it never corrects FAULTLINE. Where FAULTLINE left a question open (its §12), this document leaves
it open too, and it adds a few open questions of its own at the end.

The in-game text lives in `src/content/`. This document is what that text agrees with. Writers, artists and
composers should be able to answer "what does it look like from the car", "what was this machine for" and "how does
its task end" from here.

---

## 0. The voyage in one breath

Thirty-one years after the Night of the Fault, the KEEPER lamp at Relay Seven blinks, as it has blinked once a day
for thirty-one years. This time a crew answers it together. The Operator plugs a cord into the jack beneath the
lamp, stamps the connection with a time to live of sixteen hops, and says "Go ahead." The crew take an old cable
tender out of the Reach docks and ride the carriers along the dark outside of the Line, relay by relay: through the
rust kingdom of the Copper Reach, past the Iron Regent in the Copper Gate, through the singing violet weather of the
Glass Cathedral and the Hollow Choir, into the ember-red Blackout Heart. Behind them, the quarantine reads their
connection as storm traffic and cuts the carriers relay by relay. Ahead of them, every machine meets them as an
unknown sender. If the route holds all the way to the Core, the Core has its proof, and the shell opens.

*Every hop costs a little life.*

The Lamplighter still carries a maintenance key. Clearing a relay lets its crew open the old service locker:
cable, lenses and standard fittings worth 24 salvage in the Reach, 36 in the Cathedral and 48 near the Heart.
The stores are for the crew who restore a working connection, once per relay. Starting docks have already
provisioned the tender; guardian gates keep no such locker. The Seal requisitions the stores at relays it closes.
The chart marks recovered stores, so riding back along a quiet carrier does not make another day's supplies.

## 1. What stays true

Everything in FAULTLINE's lore: the Line held up on spires above the cloud floor; the First Shift and the Runbook;
the linefolk and their shifts, bells, greeting and belief that every message deserves an answer; the Night of the
Fault hour by hour; the four acts of care; the Silence; the Core's double bind and its daily page; the Operator and
the Night Shift; the keepers who went before; the guardians as tests; the Ground; the answer; the open questions.

Three details from FAULTLINE matter especially here:

- **Whoever answers the page becomes the keeper.** In this game the page is answered by a crew. The keeper is the
  crew as a whole. There is no avatar, no chosen one, and no bloodline.
- **Nobody is evil.** Machines keep duties. People made loving mistakes. Fights end tasks; they never kill monsters.
- **The teal-jacket woman and the Ghost are never connected in text.** She calls every evening. The Ghost's message
  arrives at the end of the queue. The game never says these two facts belong together.

And one physical fact, from FAULTLINE §1: **the Line is held up, not flown to.** Nothing crosses between relays
through the air except small drones on rotors. Everything else rides the cables, the gantries and the lifts.

## 2. The carriers, the tenders and the hop

### The carriers

The spire tops of the Line are joined by gantries, bridges and **carriers**: heavy braided cables, thick as a lift
car is wide, slung along the outside of the band from relay to relay. They carry signal in their cores and loads on
their backs. From the Ground on a clear night they are invisible; from the Line they are everything, a thousand
kilometres of copper cable gone green, sagging between spire tops over the cloud sea. Carriers meet at relays and
nowhere else.

### The tenders

Before the Fault the linefolk kept a small fleet of **cable tenders**: pressurised maintenance cars that hang from a
**drive trolley** gripping a carrier, built to mend relays and cable runs the inside crews could not reach. Inside
crews could reset a relay from its hall. When a fault sat on the outside, where no corridor ran, a tender went.
Tender crews relit guide lamps, re-spliced carrier cores, cleared snagged wreckage off the cables, towed stalled
freight back to the docks, and on the worst nights took people off broken platforms.

The **Reach docks** built them: forty-one cars over four hundred years, all to one pattern, which the dock crews
called the *lamplighter pattern* because the first job of every tender was lamps. A tender is a long brass-and-ivory
car with a **lamp cupola** at the nose (a guide lamp big enough to relight a relay's lamp from outside), a **cab
window** at the helm under the cupola, the **drive trolley and grip arms** on the roof, a **keel** of air tanks and
ballast below, and tool mounts on the roof and belly that were built for splicers and signal lamps long before
anyone bolted weapons to them. Inside, four warm-lit decks joined by a brass service lift. Coupled rear cars carry their own lifts; a sealed lift tube reaches the keel car.

Tender crews were called **lampers**. They were a proud, slightly ridiculous trade. They kept tally marks of every
lamp they relit, scratched on the inside of the helm, and argued about the numbers in bars.

After the Fault the tenders sat in the docks for thirty-one years. Over the Silence, keepers took some of them out.
Most never came back. A few are still riding (see *echo tenders*, §5).

### The hop

Relays are **switchyards**. Every relay has switchgear where its carriers meet, a **guide lamp**, and a small
brass voice that listens. A tender does not fly from relay to relay; it rides its carrier into the yard, and the
relay **switches** it onto the next carrier, and it runs down that carrier to the next relay. That is a **hop**.

The switch only moves for a connection that completes the oldest procedure in the Runbook: the greeting. The tender
calls *Hello.* The relay answers *I hear you.* The tender answers *I hear you hear me.* Then the switch throws, the
grip bites the new carrier, and the car goes. The helm sends the greeting (it has to be manned; the Runbook never
trusted a greeting to a machine alone), and the drive spools the trolley up to line speed so the car can take the
switch at a run. In a fight, a finished handshake is the way out: the switch throws, the car runs, and whatever you
were arguing with is left on the wrong carrier.

The greeting is not a key. Relays do not check who you are; they check that you said hello properly and that your
connection has hops left. That is why a tender can cross the Line when nothing else will let it through.

Relays that are dark still switch. The First Shift built the switchgear and guide lamps to run on the Line's standby
trickle, below anything the quarantine thought worth cutting. A relay with no working hall, no crew and no light in
its windows will still hear a hello and throw the switch. Nobody knows why the First Shift wanted that. The lampers
said it was so the lamps could always find their way home.

A tender can also run slowly along one carrier without switching, lamp to lamp, the way it works a fault on a single
span. It cannot leave that carrier without a relay.

### Time to live

A connection has a **hop limit**. Every relay that switches it takes one off. The linefolk call it the connection's
**TTL**, its time to live, and every Runbook procedure that sends anything anywhere stamps it with one.

On the Night of the Fault the evacuation desk marked the goodbyes *must arrive*. Must-arrive traffic carries no
TTL. That is what made the storm. Messages that could not die went round and round until the Line drowned.

The Operator stamps every keeper's connection with a TTL. Sixteen hops, stamped with the brass press on the Relay
Seven board, before she says "Go ahead." She has never explained why she will not send anything without a TTL any
more. Nobody has asked.

- **Spending TTL.** Each switch costs one. Some things cost more (a relay that makes you say hello twice).
- **Re-stamping.** The Runbook lets a relay re-stamp traffic it is willing to vouch for. Benches keep old
  dock-office stamp presses for this; markets sell stamps; some relays still re-stamp anything that says hello
  especially well. A re-stamp adds hops.
- **TTL 0.** At zero the connection is dropped. No relay will switch traffic whose time has run out. The tender
  stalls on its carrier at the last relay, lamp lit, waiting for a signal: a scavenger with a stamp to sell, an echo
  tender that shares its beam, a bench keeper who heard you on the radio. Or something worse. The Runbook calls it
  *expired in transit*.

### The Seal

The wardens' quarantine order still stands: *close every route the storm could use until a safe route is
confirmed*. To the quarantine machinery, a live connection crossing the Line relay by relay looks exactly like storm
traffic. It is a route, and it is warm.

So the quarantine follows the keeper, and it does it literally. **Cable Wraiths** crawl the carriers behind the
tender and cut them. **Quarantine Drones** close each relay the connection has crossed, in a black lattice with a
red seam of light, and hold it. The Night Shift call it **the Seal**. It is not a hunt. It is the order doing its
job, one relay at a time, patiently, from the direction you came.

- The Seal comes from behind and never stops.
- Lingering keeps a route warm, and the Seal moves faster toward warm routes.
- A sealed relay is held by quarantine drones. Its switchgear still answers a hello, but the drones read anything
  that arrives as a breach. Nothing there is worth taking.
- The Seal cannot be argued with. It can be outrun, or occasionally fooled, or drawn off by someone willing to be
  the storm for a while.

The Seal is also the most honest thing on the Line about what the voyage means. The carriers behind you are cut.
There is no way home the way you came. The only second way home is the one you build.

### Life in the car

The Line is at the edge of space. Outside the car the air is too thin and too cold to breathe; a breach vents the
car into it. The **air plant** keeps the decks breathable. **Evasion** is the drive surging, braking and swinging
the car on its carrier so that a bolt meant for the galley goes through empty air. The **ward mesh** (the lampers'
Shield Array) is a lattice of charged ward-wire around the car that catches bolts and grounds them through the
carrier; each layer that catches one has to recharge. Fires are fought with hand extinguishers and, if it comes to
it, by opening a hatch to the outside and letting the thin air take the fire. Crew walk the decks and climb the
ladders between them.

## 3. What the machines are

The Runbook's automata were built for the whole Line, inside and out. Out on the carriers they meet the tender one
of three ways.

- **Crawlers** ride or clamp onto a carrier or a gantry rail, as the tender does: recovery drones, cutters, cranes,
  coils, other tenders. They are slow to swing out of the way.
- **Installations** are built into a relay, a gate or a hall: beacon masts, hives, checkpoints, bells, foundry
  guardians, and all three guardians. They do not move. The tender fights them as its carrier passes, and they are
  heavily plated because they were built to last where they stand.
- **Fliers** are the Line's small drones on rotors and thrusters: bell-drones, cooling drones, sealing drones. They
  are the only things out there that truly fly, and they are very hard to hit.

All of them are **crewless**. Many carry **escort automatons**: small maintenance machines that walk their decks,
put out fires, patch breaches and repair systems, the way they were built to follow work crews. Some send across
boarders, along the carrier, down a grapple line or off a rotor: **spark mites** (cleaning mites that strip anything
carrying current), **splicers** (splicing rigs that cut cables in order to repair them) and, in the Heart,
**marshal troopers** (the escort detail of a Null Marshal). Boarders are not killed; they are **stopped**.

They meet every living thing as an **unknown sender**. Their keys expired within a year of the sealing. When a
machine hails the tender, it speaks Runbook: short, capitalised, procedural. Its **handshake** tells you what it is
still doing, and it is always a refusal of some kind, because the tender has no key.

Every fight happens because a machine is doing its duty at you. A cutter severs your carrier; a checkpoint refuses
you; a recovery drone tries to recover you. The crew end the task: jam it, overload it, cut its power. Beating a
machine **ends its task**. It never dies. Its lamps dim, its grip or its rotors wind down, and whatever it was
holding (a buffer of traffic, a cut cable, a condemned list) is let go. Its task-ended line is the last thing it
says.

## 4. The stages as the tender sees them

### Stage I · The Copper Reach

From the cab window, the Reach is a continent of rust hung on the edge of the sky. The ring's outer face runs off in
both directions as a plain of riveted plate gone green and orange, and the carriers sag from spire top to spire top
in loops a kilometre long, crusted with verdigris. Spire tops rise out of the cloud sea like islands of scaffold,
crowned with stacked containers and cranes that still swing on their own schedules along their gantry rails. The
**foundries** glow dull red where their furnaces were banked instead of doused. Sun-side, the copper throws a glare
that can set a room on fire. Behind the tender, where the outer relays stood, the great ring gates are broken teeth
against the stars, and the **debris** of the Night of the Fault still hangs snagged in the carriers around them,
swinging and ticking, glittering. Ahead, far off, the **Copper Gate**: a pair of gate wings closed across every
carrier, with a crowned iron bulk built into the gap.

It is rough, rusty and busy. Things still move here: cranes, leeches, the Night Shift's scavengers. The Reach was
always the loud end of the Line.

### Stage II · The Glass Cathedral

Past the Copper Gate the carriers run into violet. The Cathedral is the Line's great optical switching hall, and
from the carriers it is a range of glass: domes and naves and buttresses of violet optical glass laid along the ring
for hundreds of kilometres, so large it has its own **weather**. Glass fog pours off its flanks and hangs under the
ring. Frost grows on the panes and on the carriers. Here the carriers themselves are partly glass, optical trunks
sheathed in braid, and they ring faintly as the trolley runs. Bells hang beneath the ring in their frames like
strange fruit, some the size of a lift car.

Light makes the glass ring. Even the tender's own lamp crossing a pane sets it humming, and a volley makes the whole
flank chime. Sometimes, crossing the Cathedral, the crew hear voices in the ringing: words, whole sentences, sealed
in the glass by the Hollow Choir.

It is beautiful and it is eerie, and it is the part of the voyage people talk about afterwards.

### Stage III · The Blackout Heart

The ring thickens. The Heart is a sphere of archive machinery built around the ring like a cathedral turned inside
out: concentric galleries, cooling fins, shelving stacks the size of spires, all of it glowing **ember red** because
the Core is burning its reserves to keep the archive alive. The carriers run into it through the galleries, and the
Seal's lattice is everywhere, black with its red seams. There are **dark stretches** where the Core has pulled
every lamp's power inward, and **ember drafts** where the heat of spending comes off the cooling fins in sparks.

At the centre, through cracks in the galleries, the crew can see the **shell**, and inside the shell the **queue**:
a field of small lights, thirty-one years of messages, each one glowing, each one waiting.

It is solemn and it is urgent. The archive is spending itself while you watch.

## 5. The Night Shift out here

The Night Shift are the few hundred who did not go down (see FAULTLINE §7). Most live around Relay Seven, the
benches and the Copper Market. A few cars of theirs ride the carriers.

### The Operator

She runs the Relay Seven board. She says little. On the voyage she is sometimes on the radio, and she says less.
"Relay Seven." "Still have you." "Received." "Go ahead." She stamps every keeper's connection with a TTL. Her name
is in the Record.

### The Copper Market and Pell

The **Copper Market** is a stack of shipping containers on a Reach spire top, welded into a tower, lit by strings of
salvaged relay lamps, with stalls on every landing and a crane that the market uses as a lift. Three carriers meet
at the relay below it, and scavenger cars hang off the market's gantries like washing. Scavengers bring in what the
Reach still gives: cable, lenses, spares from powered-down escorts, parts of machines whose tasks ended. Everything
is priced in **salvage**, which in the Night Shift means whatever can be traded for something else.

**Pell** keeps the biggest stall. She is in her sixties, has a ledger for everything and a sense of humour like a
crimper. Before the Fault she was the tally clerk at Dock Twelve. On the Night of the Fault she counted people onto
the lifts, car by car, and did not count herself. She says she lost count. Nobody believes her, and nobody says so.

She owns a crimper that once belonged to A. Varga of the cable crew. Varga's last message, still in the queue,
leaves that crimper to Hobb Tallis and adds *don't lend it to Pell*. Pell will tell you she did not borrow it. She
will tell you a lot of things. She sold the Night Shift the trolley grip that got the Lamplighter riding again, and
she is still owed for part of it.

Her stall sign reads: PELL · SALVAGE · FAIR PRICES · NO LENDING.

### Scavengers

Scavengers are Night Shift crews in **salvaged cable cars** (they call them skiffs: freight cars, lift cars and
dead tenders welded together and hung from whatever trolley still works) who strip the Reach for the Copper Market.
They are not pirates. They are people who need parts more than they can afford to be kind, and who have lived
thirty-one years on what the dead machines leave. They will try to strip a tender that looks easier than a machine,
coming alongside on a parallel carrier or down the same one. When a fight goes badly they surrender, and accepting
is always an option. They remember who spared them.

The crew the voyage keeps meeting is the **Second Helping**, a skiff made of three other cars, captained by
**Moss Adair**, who stayed up when his sister went down, and crewed by the Dunmore brothers, who do not agree about
anything except Moss.

### Bench keepers

A **bench** is a lamp left on, tools in order, a kettle on the shelf, a drawer of spares sorted by what they could
still save: what keepers leave for the next keeper, usually in a relay's switch house. A few benches have people.
**Bench keepers** live there, mend what comes in, re-stamp connections with old dock-office presses, and keep a log
of who went by.

- **Hobb Tallis** keeps Bench Nine in the Reach. Cable crew, seventies, talks to his kettle, still has not got over
  the crimper.
- **Marit Seldon** keeps the Choir Bench in the Glass Cathedral. A bellmaker. She tunes the tender's systems while
  you drink her tea, and she can tell which pane rang by ear.
- **Ennis Rook** keeps the last bench before the Heart. A warden, once on Harrow's crew as a runner. He does not
  say much about the night of the order.

### Echo tenders

An **echo tender** is a keeper's tender with nobody aboard, still riding. When a keeper's crew are lost, the
tender's autopilot does what a lamper's autopilot was built to do: it runs the relighting round. But its connection
expired long ago, and no relay will switch it, so its round is one carrier long. It runs back and forth along that
carrier between the same two relays, lamp lit, relighting the same two guide lamps that the Core will pull dark
again by morning, year after year. The Seal ignores them: they are not going anywhere.

An echo tender that finds an unknown car on its carrier treats it the way a lamper treats wreckage on a cable: as a
fault to clear. Most of the time it only hails. Sometimes it fights. Its task ends when someone switches the
autopilot off or finishes the round for it. The lampers had a rule about a tender whose crew were gone: *leave the
lamp lit, or put it out yourself; don't let the dark do it.*

The echo tender the voyage keeps meeting is the ***Kittiwake***, ridden by the courier the Copper Market remembers,
the one who got as far as the Glass Cathedral. Nobody remembers the courier's name. The logbook is signed with a
small drawn lamp.

### Stalled cars

Two kinds of car are left stranded on the Line. **Lift cars** stopped wherever they were in the spires the night
the machines at the spire feet stopped knowing anyone; some had people in them who climbed out, and some had cargo,
and some are still hanging in their shafts with a lamp on. And **tenders stall** on their carriers when their TTL
runs out or their drive fails, hanging over the cloud sea, waiting.

## 6. The Lamplighter

The tender the crew ride is car **L-12, *Lamplighter***, out of **Reach Dock Twelve**. The name is painted on the dock
plate and under the cupola, in the old lampers' lettering. Every keeper paints a name of their own over it, and
the Night Shift let them, and the old name shows through the paint anyway.

**History.** The Lamplighter was built two hundred years before the Fault and ran relighting rounds in the outer
Reach for all of them. Its last chief was **Ilse Corran**, a lamper for forty-one years. On the Night of the Fault
she ran eleven trips along the carriers to the broken outer relays, taking cable crews and dock hands off platforms
that were venting air and bringing them to the lift heads, the last trip with the air plant failing and nine people
in a hold built for four. Then she brought the tender into Dock Twelve, set the brake, scratched her last tally, and
the harbour master put her on the last shuttle. The harbour master's message to her is still in the queue: *Your
berth is open for the return trip. The lights stay on.*

For thirty-one years the Lamplighter was the one tender nobody took. Its trolley grip had cracked on the eleventh
trip, and there was no grip on the Line to fit it, until this year, when Pell found one in the Reach and sold it to
the Night Shift on terms.

**What is scratched inside the helm.** Above the switch lever, cut into the brass with the point of a crimper:

- rows and rows of tally marks in fives, every lamp the Lamplighter's crews ever relit (the Night Shift counted
  them once, got 4,406, and argued about it);
- eleven relay numbers in a column, the eleven trips of the Night of the Fault;
- in capitals, **LEAVE IT LIT**, and the initials *I.C.*;
- and in newer, clumsier scratches, from the night the Night Shift fitted the grip: *grip from Pell. paid (mostly).*

**The car.** A long brass-and-ivory lead car, riveted plates, the lamp cupola at the nose, the cab window at the helm, the
drive trolley and its grip arms on the roof, a keel of tanks and ballast below, tool mounts on the roof and belly.
Inside, three decks: the drive at the tail, the air plant, a hall, a galley with a kettle, a bench infirmary, a
listening post, the ward mesh's charge room, holds, a corridor that everyone bumps their head in, and the helm up in
the nose under the lamp. Its systems are named the way the lampers named them: Shield Array (the ward mesh),
Thrusters (the drive trolley's motors; lampers never stopped calling them that), Weapons Bay (the tool mounts),
Air Plant, Bench Infirmary, Helm, Listening Post, Bulkheads (doors and hatches), and, if you can buy them, a Drone
Bay and a Lamp-Dark Veil.

### Consists, belly cars and refits

The Reach docks never built a tender as one car. They built **consists**: coupled cars hanging from one carrier,
each on its own small trolley, made up for the job at hand and broken up again when it was done.

- The **lead car** carries the cab and helm at the nose, the drive trolley on the roof, the tool mounts and the
  life of the tender: air, mesh, infirmary, listening post. The Lamplighter is a lead car, and it has two **socket
  holds**.
- A **rear car** couples behind it through a gangway door: a **drone car** (a rotor hatch and racks), an **armory
  car** (a warden escort car with an extra mount), a **freight car**, a **bunk car** for long rounds, or a **veil
  car** (soot-black, lamps on shutters, for relays that misfire at the sight of a lit tender).
- A **keel car** is slung beneath the lead car on hangers and reached by a hatch and a ladder from the lower deck.
  Every lamper calls it the **belly car**: a **ballast keel** (the crew call it the cellar), a **listening keel** full
  of horns, a **sling keel** with a belly mount, or a **workshop keel**.
- Every coupled car is more mass on the carrier. The consist swings slower, and a machine has more rooms to aim at.

**Refits.** Every hold is a **socket**: brass rails and a fixed plug board, so that a module crate can be swung in on
a hoist and bolted down in a night. **Modules**: a drone bay or a veil housing (when no car carries those systems), a
workshop, bunks, a cargo hold, a payload rack, ballast tanks, a listening horn array, or a kettle bench.

The Night Shift refit the way they do everything: at a bench, with the kettle on, from whatever the last crew left
in the drawer. Their habits:

- They stencil the date on every crate, and the name of whoever bolted it in.
- They never refit on an empty kettle.
- They leave the old module on the shelf for the next shift, labelled with what was wrong with it.
- They argue about livery. The lamp colour of a tender (amber, teal, violet, ember or ivory) is the only thing on it
  that is purely for the crew, and every crew changes it at the first bench they reach.
- Derelict cars are everywhere on the carriers, uncoupled and stalled where their tenders left them. A crew that
  finds one with a working trolley couples it and says thank you to whoever it belonged to.

## 7. The crew

The first crew are Night Shift volunteers who stood up when the Operator asked. More join on the way: people freed
from cold berths, people taken out of stuck lift cars and stalled cars, escort automatons re-keyed on the spot, a
bellmaker who stayed behind in the Cathedral. The crew are the keeper.

| Species | Who they are |
| --- | --- |
| **Linefolk** | The people of the Line who stayed: engineers, dock hands, cooks, clerks, teachers without classes. Worn work coats and overalls in chipped ivory and dark brass, a headset with a boom mic, a small teal or amber lamp on the chest, a tool belt. They learn any station fast, because for thirty-one years they have had to. |
| **Wardens** | The Night Shift's wardens: a few of Harrow's gate crews who would not leave the gate, and the ones they trained, from sixteen, as the order says. Dark armour plates, a brass visor band, an ember stripe. Slow, hard to burn, hard to stop. Good at the ward mesh: they were trained to hold a boundary. |
| **Riggers** | Reclaimed escort automatons. When a machine's task ends, its escorts power down. The Night Shift found that an escort wiped back to its first page trusts the first voice that greets it properly (the Runbook calls it the *first hello*), and they have been re-keying escorts that way for years. Squat ivory shells, one teal lens, tool arms, no face. They do not breathe, cannot use a bench infirmary, repair anything twice as fast as a person and fight half as well. Each has a designation (a serial and a bird the Night Shift picked, like *Rigger 7-Tern*). They remember nothing from before their first hello, except sometimes, a route. |
| **Couriers** | Message runners. Before the Fault they carried what the Line could not: signed papers, keys, parcels, the occasional apology in person. The Night Shift's couriers still run messages by hand between the last lamps, along the gantries and down the carriers hand over hand when they have to. Light long coats, satchels, goggles pushed up, amber lamps. Fast on their feet and faster at a helm. |
| **Bellmakers** | The ones who stayed in the Cathedral. A handful of bellmakers never left the choir loft, and have spent thirty-one years tuning bells that nobody else hears. Long violet-trimmed coats, glass tuning forks, a glass pendant. Fragile. A bellmaker can **tune** a system: sit in its room, listen, and find it a little more power than it had. They only join if they choose to. |

Hostile boarders (machines) are **spark mites**, **splicers** and, in the Heart, **marshal troopers**. They are
stopped, never killed.

## 8. The machines of the voyage

Every hostile, its mobility class, what it was, what duty it keeps now, and how its task ends. Handshake lines and
task-ended lines are in `src/content/flavor.ts`.

### Stage I · The Copper Reach

- **Packet Leech** · *crawler*. *Was:* a recovery drone that crawled the outer carriers gathering stray traffic from
  their cores and carried it to the Hollow Exchange for redelivery. *Now:* the exchange stopped answering; its buffer
  tank is full to bursting and glowing amber; it clamps onto anything live on its carrier and drains its power to
  keep from losing a single packet. *Task ends:* its buffer is released; the traffic it held goes out into the
  tender's outbound queue, and the leech hangs light and still on its carrier.
- **Cable Wraith** · *crawler*. *Was:* one of the isolation cutters sent crawling along the carriers in the eleventh
  hour to cut every route the storm could travel. *Now:* it never received the stand-down, and it follows live
  routes the way it was built to, shears first. Wraiths are the Seal's cutters. *Task ends:* the shears fold; for
  once, a new cable stays connected.
- **Rust Prophet** · *installation* (beacon mast). *Was:* a beacon on a tall mast over a relay yard that broadcast
  corrosion warnings so crews could get there before the rust did. *Now:* with nobody coming, the warnings got
  louder; its horns now broadcast on the same bands as the thing it warns about, and ionise everything that passes.
  *Task ends:* the horns go quiet; the warning lamp stops turning.
- **Scrap Foreman** · *crawler* (gantry crane). *Was:* the crane that ran a relay yard's inspections along its gantry
  rail and hauled condemned hardware to the smelters. *Now:* it still rolls the yard for an inspection nobody
  scheduled, condemns what it finds, and never files the paperwork that would spare it. It lobs payloads the way it
  used to drop scrap into a skip. *Task ends:* the hammer rests; the condemned list goes unsigned.
- **Scavenger Skiff** · *crawler* (salvaged cable car, human crew). Night Shift scavengers from the Copper Market.
  Not machines, not villains. They can surrender. *Their fight ends:* when they give up, or run.
- **Static Nest** · *installation* (hive under a relay platform). *Was:* a brood chamber for small retry drones, each
  of which holds one line open until the exchange answers. *Now:* the exchange has not answered since the Fault. It
  hatches spark mites and sends them down the carriers to hold lines on anything that has lines. *Task ends:* the
  nest goes quiet; every line it held is let go.
- **Ferric Colossus** · *installation* (elite, foundry guardian). *Was:* the guardian built into a Reach foundry's
  wall to keep people away from the furnace mouth. *Now:* it guards a furnace that was banked, not doused, and trusts
  only iron and the redundant safety circuits its makers left it. *Task ends:* the furnace door settles; even iron can
  learn to rest.

**Guardian I · The Iron Regent** · *installation*. The Iron Regent **is** the Copper Gate: the border machine built
into the gate frame, the gateway that decided which routes from outside were allowed onto the inner Line. A crowned
armoured bulk set into the gap between two gate wings, with the carriers running through it. Its tarnished brass
crown is its routing authority. When the ring broke it closed its wings across every carrier, and it has held them
closed since. Its **Gate Wardens** are pieces of the gate itself, drones that unfold from its wings on rotors when
it rises.

*Its test:* **no passage without proof of a second way home.** The Regent opens only for a route that can lose any
one road and still arrive. It does not want to be beaten. It wants to see the tender lose a road and keep coming:
lose a system and fight on, lose the mesh and route around it. A route that survives the Regent has a second way
home by definition. *Task ends:* the crown dims to the colour of old brass, the gate wings open, the carriers run on
through, and beyond them the glass bells are ringing.

### Stage II · The Glass Cathedral

- **Prism Widow** · *crawler* (on glass-web cables). *Was:* the automaton that repaired the Cathedral's glass
  circuits, spinning new optical threads across cracked panes. *Now:* it weaves isolation webs of light around the
  last working signals, perfectly, so that nothing can get through. *Task ends:* the web unravels; light takes the
  long way home.
- **Glass Echo** · *flier*. *Was:* a small bell-drone on rotors that carried a bell's note to the far naves. *Now:* it
  repeats the last order it heard, louder as it cracks, and it is very hard to hit. *Task ends:* the bell cracks
  through; the last echo goes out and does not come back.
- **Wire Weaver** · *crawler*. *Was:* the Hollow Exchange's wiring automaton, sent up to rewire the bell frames.
  *Now:* it cannot tell a repair from a snare, and it keeps adding tension until every line is ready to snap; it
  launches small drones to hold the ends. *Task ends:* the threads slacken.
- **Glass Choir** · *installation* (three bells on a frame). *Was:* three bells that rang the evacuation order across
  a nave. *Now:* each repeats a different fragment of the order, and they never agree; there is no quorum. *Task
  ends:* for a moment, all three bells agree on silence.
- **Coil Serpent** · *crawler*. *Was:* a segmented coil that wound up dead cable for reuse. *Now:* it tightens around
  anything that still carries a signal. *Task ends:* the coils open.
- **Echo Tender** · *crawler* (a keeper's tender on autopilot). See §5. Nobody aboard, lamp lit, running its
  one-carrier round. It hails more often than it fights. *Task ends:* the autopilot lets go of the round, and its
  lamp is either left lit or put out by the crew, as the lampers' rule says.

**Guardian II · The Hollow Choir** · *installation*. The Cathedral's announcement engine, hung on chains in the great
glass hall. During the Fault it repeated the evacuation order until the glass memorised it. Then the voices stopped
arriving: messages marked for the Cathedral that were never answered. The Choir gathers every unanswered voice and
seals it in a bell, because its job was to make sure every announcement was heard, and a voice sealed in glass can
still be heard. Violet glass bells, porcelain masks and organ-pipe spines. Its Choristers each hold one voice they
will not release.

*Its test:* **plurality breaks the glass.** One voice is an echo; many voices can break the glass. A single weapon
striking alone only makes a bell ring. Many hits landing together (a volley, beams and bolts and payloads arriving
at once) break it. The Choir will only let a voice go when more than one voice is speaking. *Task ends:* the masks
open their mouths, and this time the voices leave.

### Stage III · The Blackout Heart

- **Gate Sentinel** · *installation* (checkpoint). *Was:* a checkpoint built over the carriers at the archive's trust
  boundary, with a key-scanner eye and barred gates. *Now:* it checks keys that expired more than ten thousand days
  ago and finds every visitor a stranger. *Task ends:* the checkpoint releases its lock; the archive remains intact.
- **Null Marshal** · *crawler*. *Was:* the armoured escort car that took keyed engineers safely across the trust
  boundary. *Now:* no keyed engineers exist, so it bars the people it was built to protect, and it sends marshal
  troopers down the carrier to escort intruders out. *Task ends:* the warrant expires.
- **Ash Moth** · *flier*. *Was:* a cooling drone on rotors that followed heat to the relays that needed it. *Now:* its
  radiator-fin wings are ruined and shed conductive ash, and it starts fires in everything it tries to cool. *Task
  ends:* the wings fold around a lantern that no longer needs tending.
- **Grave Reaver** · *crawler*. *Was:* the machine that crawled out to dead reactors and took them apart so their
  parts could be used again. *Now:* it hears every weak signal as permission to begin. *Task ends:* the claws lower.
- **Demolition Engine** · *crawler* (elite). *Was:* a decommissioning machine sent down the carriers to bring down a
  relay the wardens sealed. *Now:* the recall never reached it, and it counts down on everything it meets. *Task
  ends:* the countdown lamp dims to nothing; the relay it came for will stand another night.
- **Quarantine Drone** · *flier* (the Seal's sealing drone, every stage). Black shell, red seam light, four rotors.
  *Was and is:* the sealing drones that closed the shell, and now close and hold every relay the keeper's connection
  crosses. *Task ends:* the clamps open; somewhere behind the shell, a route is marked safe.

**Guardian III · The Blackout Core** · *installation*. The Heart's custodian, built into the shell, following the
last order it received. It sent for you. It has to stop you. Both are true.

*Its test:* **custody, emergency, event horizon.** It defends in the order the Runbook prescribes for a quarantine.

1. **Custody.** Cut, breach, jam, strike, in rotation. The quarantine as written.
2. **Emergency.** At half integrity it spends reserve it was keeping for the archive: more power, faster cycles. The
   ember light goes white at the edges. Every lamp in the Heart dims to feed it.
3. **Event Horizon.** It pulls every light in the room inward, even the tender's own lamp, and raises its sealing
   drones to close the shell for good.

A route that survives all three is a safe route by definition. The Core cannot be ordered to open. It can only be
shown a route that holds. *Task ends:* the isolation shell falls silent. Inside it, the delivery lights are still
on. Defeating the Core breaks the isolation machinery, never the archive.

## 9. Message fragments and the queue

The queue holds the evacuation's last traffic and thirty-one years of messages sent up from the Ground. As the
guardians weaken and as the crew pull traffic out of leeches, relays and wrecks, **fragments** leak: real packets,
each small and specific. There are three kinds.

- **Queue.** From the evacuation: arrival notices, apologies, shift swaps, repair requests, a music box held for
  collection, a harbour master keeping a berth open.
- **Ground.** Sent up from below over thirty-one years: births, weather, doors that will not open, children who want
  to know if the Line is real.
- **Evening calls** (*Unknown sender · priority low*). A woman on the Ground at a radio built from a lift panel,
  every evening at the same time. She never says who she is calling. She tries again tomorrow.

The crew can also carry things for the queue: a parcel, a letter, a reply. Carried things arrive with the rest.

## 10. The ending

When the Blackout Core's task ends, a safe route is confirmed at last.

The shell opens. Thirty-one years of messages leave at once. They race along the carriers from the Heart, through
the Glass Cathedral, which rings with all of them, through the Copper Gate, down every spire and through the cloud
floor. The lamps of the Line come back on segment by segment, and the Faultline closes. The arrival notices arrive.
The apology is heard. The music box notice is delivered. The harbour master's berth is open.

Below, the clouds are thin that night. People come out of the lift-car houses and stand in the rain to watch the gap
in the sky fill with light. At the usual time, the woman at the radio keys it, and for the first time something
comes back: first the queue, then a live acknowledgement from the Line. She answers. The game shows only that there
was an answer. *And then, an answer.*

At the very end of the queue, one short message is delivered. The board shows it went through. *Made it down. Your
turn.* The game does not say whom it was for.

At Relay Seven, on the Operator's board, a lamp that nobody living has ever seen lit begins to glow. Its engraved
label reads **GROUND**. The Operator looks at it for a long time. Then she lifts the headset.

Things the crew carried arrive with the queue, and the ending remembers them (the music box, Pell's letter, the
courier's log, a reply to the evening caller).

**Game over.** When the tender is lost, the line goes quiet. If the car breaks up, the lamp is the last thing to go
out. If the crew are lost and the car is not, its autopilot finds the nearest guide lamp and begins the relighting
round, and there is one more echo tender on the Line. Either way, the Seal closes over the last relay the connection
touched. At Relay Seven the cord is cold in the jack. The next day the lamp blinks again, and the Operator lifts the
headset, looks at whoever is in the room, and asks, "Who'll take it?"

## 11. Things no one knows

Everything in FAULTLINE §12 stays open: what broke the outer relays; who the First Shift were; what is upstream;
what *Handover* means; who the Operator is; what the woman at the radio says; what the Ghost looks like. Characters
may argue about them. No text settles them.

This game adds a few of its own:

- **Why the switchgear runs below the quarantine.** The First Shift built the switchgear and guide lamps to work on
  a trickle nothing else could use, and to switch for anyone who says hello. Nobody knows what they expected to need
  that for.
- **Why the Operator stamps a TTL.** She has never said. People who have thought about it long enough have a guess,
  and keep it to themselves.
- **Whether Pell really lost count.**
- **Where a rigger's routes come from.** Riggers remember nothing from before the first hello, except sometimes a
  route they cannot explain, which turns out to be right.
- **Whether the voices in the Cathedral glass are recordings.** The bellmakers say a voice sealed in glass is still a
  voice. Nobody has proved them wrong.

## 13. Writing the voyage

FAULTLINE's "Writing the Line" applies in full: understate; nobody is evil; machines keep duties; no chosen ones;
tech is ancient and half understood; ordinary messages carry the weight; leave the open questions open; no
exclamation marks; no swords, torches or scrolls.

This game adds:

- **Nothing flies but drones.** The tender rides carriers, is switched at relays, stalls when its TTL runs out.
  Other cars come alongside on parallel carriers or down the same one. Installations are passed, not approached
  through open space. Only drones on rotors fly. No spaceships, docking tubes, orbits, planets or asteroids.
- **It may be warmer and funnier.** Night-Shift gallows humour, FTL's dry fatalism. People joke because the
  alternative is worse. The jokes are small and human: a kettle, a debt, a bad pun on a dock plate.
- **Machines speak Runbook.** Short, capitalised, procedural, precise about numbers. They are never menacing on
  purpose. They are only doing their job at you.
- **Each stage sounds different.** Stage I is rough, rusty, loud and funny: scavengers, cranes, leeches, Pell.
  Stage II is beautiful and eerie: bells, fog, voices in the glass, a bellmaker's tea. Stage III is solemn and
  urgent: the archive burning its reserves, the queue glowing, wardens who remember the order.
- **The crew is the keeper.** Write "the crew", "you", or a named crew member. Never a single hero.
- **Losses are fair.** When a choice can go badly, the text says so before the choice, in a way a careful reader
  would catch.
